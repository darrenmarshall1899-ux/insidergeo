Yemen Explained: How the Houthis Ended Up Sitting on the World's Other Oil Chokepoint

GeoInsider · Sep 20, 2026 · Explainer

If you stopped following Yemen years ago, this one is for you. Who's fighting, why it suddenly matters to people nowhere near the Red Sea, what the Houthis can actually do with the weapons they have, and where I think this realistically goes.

Most people I talk to filed Yemen away around 2018 under "sad, stuck war" and honestly I can't blame them, for years that was a fair description. It stopped being fair this month. In about three days in early September the Houthis took nearly the whole Red Sea coast of Yemen plus the islands at the mouth of the Bab el-Mandeb strait, and they did it at a moment when the Strait of Hormuz was already mostly shut. So I'm going to start from the beginning.

Who is fighting who

The Houthis call themselves Ansar Allah. They're an armed movement from Yemen's northern highlands, and they've held the capital Sanaa and a big chunk of the north and west since 2014. Iran backs them. How much Tehran actually gives them orders is an old argument and I'm not going to settle it in this piece.

On the other side is the internationally recognised government, which sits in Aden under something called the Presidential Leadership Council. I say "government forces" in this article because it's the easy shorthand, but in reality it's a patchwork of armies with different sponsors and different histories. There's the regular army around Taiz and Marib, the Giants Brigades, the Nation's Shield forces, tribal fighters, southern separatists, and Tareq Saleh's National Resistance, which held the west coast up until this month. What they have in common is the enemy, and not a lot else.

Saudi Arabia supports that camp with air power and money. The UAE used to be the most effective outside player on the west coast, but it ended its involvement at the end of last year and pulled its troops out. That left a gap, and as you'll see the gap mattered.

How we got here

A UN-brokered truce in April 2022 more or less held for four years. During the Gaza war the Houthis turned their attention to shipping instead. Between late 2023 and mid-2025 they attacked more than a hundred commercial ships they said were linked to Israel or its allies, and in July 2025 they sank two of them and killed four seafarers.

Then the regional war came. The US and Israel attacked Iran on 28 February 2026, Iran and its allies hit back across the region and the Houthis joined in a month later. Hormuz has been largely closed to normal traffic ever since.

The Yemeni truce fell apart in July. According to Reuters-sourced accounts an Iranian flight carrying IRGC personnel and weapons tried to land at Sanaa, the runway was bombed to stop it, and the Houthis declared the ceasefire over. On 20 July they announced a blockade on shipping tied to Saudi ports. Three days later they claimed strikes on two Saudi tankers, and Riyadh confirmed one of them had been hit. By August they were hitting government camps with ballistic missiles and drones.

The ground offensive started in western Taiz on 3 September. Between 9 and 11 September the Houthis took Hays, al-Khokha, Mocha and Dhubab, got onto the Zuqar and Hanish islands and landed on Mayun (also called Perim), which is the island sitting in the strait itself.

Why did the coast go so fast? Reuters' sources gave four reasons: the anti-Houthi factions were divided, the Saudi led command structure that replaced the Emiratis had never been tested, Saudi air support was limited, and everybody underestimated the Houthi build-up. One diplomat put Houthi strength at about 100,000 fighters, which I have no way of checking.

Why anyone outside Yemen should care

Bab el-Mandeb is the southern gate of the Red Sea, so it's also the southern gate of the Suez Canal. Roughly 12% of global trade goes through it, along with 11% of seaborne oil and 8% of LNG. If ships avoid it they have to go around Africa, and that can add about 22 days to a voyage.

Bab el-Mandeb by the numbers

11% of seaborne oil
8% of LNG

3.5m bpd Yanbu exports in June, up from 240,000 a year earlier
8.1m bpd carried by the strait in Q2, almost double pre-crisis

In a normal year that would already be a big deal. This year it's worse, because with Hormuz shut Saudi Arabia has been sending its oil west by pipeline to Yanbu on the Red Sea and shipping it out from there. Exports from Yanbu through Bab el-Mandeb went from about 240,000 barrels a day a year earlier to 3.5 million in June. The strait as a whole carried around 8.1 million barrels a day in the second quarter, almost double what it was before. So the route the world was using to get around Hormuz now goes right past Houthi-held coast.

And the bypass got hit too. A drone attack launched from Iraq damaged three pumping stations on the East-West pipeline and forced a temporary shutdown. Brent went back above $100 when Mocha fell, touched $109, and was somewhere around $103 to $105 last week, with Aramco reportedly expecting to get half the pipeline's flow back. A former State Department official called simultaneous Iranian leverage over both straits "the worst-case scenario."

If you live in Europe, what that means in practice is fuel prices, freight costs and things arriving late. There's a military side as well. The Houthi advance has put them within striking distance of the big US base in Djibouti, just across the water, and one report has their nearest positions about 32 kilometres from Camp Lemonnier. People are crossing the strait too. More than 2,000 Yemenis reached Djibouti in a matter of days.

What they have

I'll try to keep this practical because a list of missile names doesn't really tell you anything.

Start with the anti-ship missiles. The Houthis have both ballistic and cruise types, including Chinese-designed C-802s. The ballistic ones reach furthest, up to about 300 km, but they need somebody to tell them where the ship is, and that means drones, boats or Iranian help. It's also worth knowing that some of the scarier missiles they've paraded looked like mockups to outside analysts. Where they are strong is in being able to threaten ships from the coast at lots of different ranges. Hitting precisely is a different question.

Then drones. They have one-way attack drones in large numbers, and some are reportedly built for as little as $1,500 from smuggled parts, with Iranian engineering help and Chinese components. They use them to strike and they use them to find targets. The problem for the other side is that a drone that cheap still has to be shot down with something expensive.

The ones that worry me more are the explosive boats and the mines. Nobody writes headlines about mines, but they can mess up shipping with almost no effort from whoever lays them.

Uncrewed explosive boats cost very little compared to the defences you need to stop them. And the Houthis have shown off several types of sea mine at their parades.

For long range they have ballistic missiles and drones that reach deep into Saudi Arabia. This month they hit Abha, Khamis Mushait, Jizan and Najran and wounded 73 people, and now they've claimed attacks on Riyadh and on an Aramco site at Yanbu.

And then there's manpower, which I think gets overlooked. One analysis I found convincing argues that the Houthis' real weapon is mobilisation, meaning years spent building a big, organised infantry force under central command. The same piece points out that Israeli strikes killed senior figures including their chief of staff, and that interdiction has squeezed their supply lines. So they've taken real damage. They've just absorbed it better than most people expected.

What they can and can't do with all that

First thing, the strait isn't a gate. The main shipping channel is about 16 miles wide and 650 feet deep, nobody is going to physically close it.

What the Houthis can do is make it too risky or too expensive to use, and they can be selective about who they do it to. That's exactly what they did from 2023 to 2025 and what they've been doing to Saudi-linked cargo since July. Holding the coast and the islands helps them in a few ways. The distance to their targets gets shorter and ships get less warning. They also get options they didn't really have before, like mines and direct fire, and they can now watch the southern approaches from positions that weren't theirs a month ago.

The shipping industry is nervous but it isn't panicking. Reuters counted 21 vessels crossing on Wednesday. IMF PortWatch had 31 transits on 13 September against 43 on the same day a year ago. Some container lines are even resuming Suez services, which surprised me a bit.

They have real limits though. They don't have a navy or an air force worth the name, and holding exposed islands while the Saudis bomb you is hard. Al Jazeera made the same point about Mayun. On top of that their new coastal strip sits underneath mountains they don't control, and that's where the ground war comes in.

Where the fighting is now

Once the coast fell the war moved up into the hills between Taiz and Lahj. The feature that matters most is the Kahboub range, just east of the strait. Yemen Monitor says it has seen the fiercest fighting since Mocha fell, and the Houthis haven't been able to take it or push east past it.

So they've been probing further east instead. Over the weekend they reportedly broke into several villages in Lahj's al-Mudharabah district, and the Giants sent a brigade in response. On Sunday the government side claimed it had retaken most of Jabal Numan, with fighting still going on.

Sourcing caveat

Keep in mind that nearly all of that comes from government-side sources, and the Houthis haven't commented. I went through it in detail in my last thread. The short version is that the fast phase looks to be over for now, and what's left is a slow fight over high ground.

What the West is doing

Directly, not very much so far. Mohammed bin Salman reportedly called Trump twice asking for US strikes and Trump said no. CNN reports the US has stepped up intelligence and targeting support for the Saudis but has drawn the line at offensive strikes. The State Department has warned Americans that things could escalate quickly, and the EU's Aspides naval mission has raised its alert level in the southern Red Sea. Saudi Arabia announced a multinational maritime coalition back in July. Pakistan has a defence pact with Riyadh, and for now it says no military response is being discussed.

What could realistically happen

These are my guesses, roughly in order of how likely I think they are.

The most likely one is entrenchment. The Houthis keep the coast and the islands and dig in, the fighting in the hills grinds on, and they keep squeezing Saudi-linked shipping as leverage. Ibrahim Jalal, a Yemeni analyst whose work I rate, argues that their priority right now is consolidation and that every week they hold the coast makes it more expensive to take back. I put this first mainly because it doesn't require anybody to do anything new.

Second is a Houthi breakthrough in the hills. If they take Kahboub and the heights to the east of it, their coastal strip is secure, Lahj comes under pressure and the road linking Taiz to Aden is threatened. At that point the government's interim capital is at risk. I don't think it's about to happen, but the al-Mudharabah report is a reminder that it could.

Third, a real counteroffensive to retake the coast. The government says it will do this. For it to work the anti-Houthi factions would have to actually cooperate, and they'd need far more air support than they got in September, which happen to be the two things that were missing when the coast fell. I'd want to see a ridgeline change hands and stay changed before I believe it.

The last one is wider escalation, and it's the one that drags the West in. I can think of a few triggers: a mass-casualty strike on Riyadh, a hit on a US ship or on the base in Djibouti, or a serious mining of the strait. Any one of those could change the calculation in Washington very quickly.

All four are tied to the Iran war. If there's a deal on Hormuz a lot of the Houthis' leverage goes away with it. If there isn't, both chokepoints stay under pressure at the same time, and for me that's the really dangerous part.

What I'm watching

On the ground it's Kahboub and the heights running east to Jirdad. At sea I'm looking at the daily transit numbers and for any report of mines. And in Washington the question is whether "no offensive strikes" survives the next attack on Riyadh. I'll update when any of those move.
