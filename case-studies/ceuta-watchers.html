If the Watchers Were Real: Reading the Ceuta Intelligence Claim

Spanish outlet reports that Moroccan intelligence officers were in the crowds that entered Ceuta, there to watch how Spain responded.

GeoInsider · Aug 2, 2026 · Analysis

Between 30 and 31 July, around 50,000 people entered the Spanish city of Ceuta from Morocco by the government's count. The city's own government puts the figure closer to 60,000. Ceuta has about 84,000 residents, which means that in under 48 hours the city absorbed a number of arrivals approaching two thirds of its population. For scale, the May 2021 crisis that froze Spanish-Moroccan relations for a year involved eight to ten thousand people over two days, and it was treated at the time as an unprecedented event.

The human cost is still being counted, and the counting itself has become a dispute between Spanish institutions. The central government's official toll stands at 72. Ceuta's president, Juan Jesús Vivas, told El País that the city morgue has received 88 bodies, in facilities expanded to hold 200. Moroccan authorities are recovering more from the sea on their side. Some of the dead were not victims of these crossings at all but of earlier attempts, washed up in the same days. Nobody can currently say how many people drowned trying to swim around the Tarajal breakwater last week, and it is possible nobody ever will.

Spain deployed the army within hours, put roughly 3,000 soldiers and police on the streets, and by Saturday was anchoring a 500-metre floating barrier off Tarajal. Pedro Sánchez called the entries an attack on Spain's territorial integrity. Returns to Morocco began almost immediately under existing readmission arrangements and passed 48,000 within days, by the Interior Ministry's count.

Then, on 2 August, El Español published the claim this essay is about. Citing anonymous sources in the Guardia Civil and the Spanish intelligence services, the paper reported that officers monitoring the border camera network in real time recognised individuals linked to Moroccan intelligence among the crowds, early on the first day, and that these individuals were there to observe Spain's operational response rather than to cross.

Evidentiary status

This is one outlet. The sources are anonymous. Neither the Interior Ministry nor the Guardia Civil has said anything on the record. Rabat has not responded. Every other outlet carrying the story is reproducing El Español, which means the republication you are seeing is one source chain wearing twenty mastheads. Since publication, specifics have been bolted on downstream that the original article does not contain, including a headcount for the alleged officers and descriptions of what they were supposedly there to do. Those additions are circulating through nationalist Spanish outlets and Russian-linked aggregation, and you should discount them entirely. There is even a loose thread inside the original report itself: the confirming camera detail is attributed to the perimeter at Beni Enzar, which is the Melilla crossing, four hundred kilometres away. That may be a slip. It may also mean the sourcing mixes two borders. Either way it is the kind of detail that should keep your confidence modest.

The honest framing is not "Morocco infiltrated Ceuta." It is "a credible Spanish outlet reports that Spanish officers believe they recognised Moroccan intelligence personnel on their cameras." What follows is a reading of what it would mean if that belief turns out to be correct.

What is actually being claimed, and what is not

The scenario the report supports is narrow: officers present in the crowds, under tasking, to collect on how Spain responded, without Spain's consent. That is an intelligence operation conducted on allied territory, and it would be a serious matter.

What the report does not independently establish is the much larger claim, that Morocco organised or directed the crossings themselves. Some of the Spanish sources quoted in the article gesture toward that reading, framing the week as hybrid pressure that could not have happened without the involvement of Moroccan services. That is their inference, and it may even be right, but the published evidence does not carry it on its own. Observation and organisation are different allegations with different burdens of proof, and a reader who merges them is doing Rabat's accusers' work for free. Throughout this piece, the conditional being analysed is the first claim only.

It is worth adding that the vacuum is filling fast. A theory circulating widely ties the crisis to Sánchez's meeting with Algeria's president in Algiers about eleven days earlier, on the logic that Rabat was punishing the reconciliation. The Spanish government denies the link and no public evidence supports it. Another strand blames a recent Supreme Court ruling restricting summary returns of migrants intercepted at sea, which smuggling networks allegedly spun into an invitation. Sánchez himself has pointed at that mechanism. None of this is established, and the speed at which unsupported specifics are hardening into accepted background is itself part of the story.

Why recognition is plausible, and why that cuts both ways

There is a documented reason Spanish officers could genuinely recognise Moroccan intelligence personnel on a screen, and it was published two weeks before the crisis by people with no stake in it.

On 16 July, Forbidden Stories and its partners released a major investigation into Morocco's security services, built partly on a former officer of the DGST, Morocco's domestic intelligence service, speaking under a pseudonym. Alongside the Pegasus revelations that got the headlines, the consortium documented something quieter: years of close cooperation between the Guardia Civil and the DGST. Spanish personnel trained their Moroccan counterparts in the intelligence and surveillance methods used in Spain. The two services ran joint exercises, including in Fnideq, the Moroccan town directly across the Ceuta border, and around Tangier. DGST officers were hosted in Ceuta and on the Spanish mainland. The relationship dates to the early 2010s and was solid enough by 2017 that internal photographs show the officers socialising. As recently as the first days of July this year, the head of the DGST was receiving a senior Guardia Civil intelligence delegation.

If Spanish border analysts recognised faces in that crowd, this history explains how. These would not be strangers matched against a database. Some of them would be men the Spanish officers had trained, exercised with, and eaten dinner with.

But the same history is exactly why recognition proves less than it seems to. Fnideq is the town the crossings came from. DGST officers live and work in that area because managing that border is their job. The gap between "we saw men we know" and "those men were sent to collect on us" is the entire question, and nothing published so far closes it.

What watching would actually buy

Suppose the tasking claim holds. It is worth asking what the observers would have gained, because the answer narrows the story further.

The macro picture required no officers at all. The whole world watched the perimeter and the Tarajal breakwater saturate under volume on live television, watched Spain need the army within hours, watched the floating barrier go in days later. Any analyst in Rabat with a television now knows Ceuta's static defences fail at scale and that Spain's answer to failure is mass military deployment.

What cameras do not carry is the micro detail. How long the chain of command took between alarm, authorisation and deployment. Where the decision points sat and who held them. How the Guardia Civil, the National Police and the armed forces divided the ground and where the seams between them were. Which sensors and staging areas exist, which blind spots, how units rotated as the hours ran on, what procedures degraded under saturation. That is real intelligence and a professional service would want it. It is also tactical and procedural in nature, which makes the operation, if it happened, a much more specific and less cinematic thing than the word "infiltration" suggests.

Madrid's empty toolbox

The reflex reading is that confirmation would wreck Spanish-Moroccan relations. I don't believe it, because Spain has almost nothing to escalate with, and we have already watched Madrid demonstrate this under worse provocation.

The structural facts first. Morocco holds the strongest lever over how much migratory pressure reaches the fences of Ceuta and Melilla, a lever whose effect was just demonstrated in both directions within a single week. Moroccan services also feed Spain counterterrorism cooperation that Spanish governments of both colours have described as indispensable, cooperation that has contributed to disrupting plots and producing arrests in Spain and in France. Most unilateral measures available to Madrid, from expulsions to formal protest, would trigger immediate reciprocal costs in exactly these two areas, and the costs would land on Spain faster than on Morocco.

Now the precedent. Spanish prosecutors established that Sánchez's own phone was infected with Pegasus spyware five times across 2020 and 2021, with more than 2.5 gigabytes of material taken. The defence minister's phone was accessed four times over five months. The interior and agriculture ministers were targeted too. Press reporting, citing classified Spanish intelligence documents, pointed at the entourage of the Moroccan crown as the origin of the order, though the Spanish state never formalised that attribution. And what did Madrid do about a confirmed, sustained, far more intrusive operation against the head of its own government? It shelved the judicial investigation in 2023, reopened it in 2024 when French developments forced its hand, and let it die again. No expulsions. No suspension of anything. The 2022 realignment with Rabat proceeded while the infections were being counted.

That is revealed behaviour, and it is the single best predictor available for how Spain would respond to confirmation of a comparatively modest observation operation. Quietly.

The ledger that would actually reopen

Which is why I think the real consequence of confirmation would be domestic, and Sánchez knows it.

In March 2022, Spain abandoned decades of formal neutrality on Western Sahara and endorsed Morocco's autonomy plan. The decision blew up relations with Algeria, then Spain's principal gas supplier, and was sold at home on one argument: it bought a stable southern border and a reliable partner. Migration control, counterterrorism, commerce, calm. That was the price and that was the product.

The product is now in question in the most public way possible. The border just produced the largest mass entry in Spanish history and a death toll the two levels of Spanish government cannot agree on, with the gap between 72 and 88 bodies sitting in a morgue expanded to triple capacity. Confirmation that Moroccan intelligence officers were inside the event, watching Spain flail, would convert a policy embarrassment into a policy indictment. The opposition does not need to build the argument. It has been building it since 2022, and it would simply attach this to it. My read is that this, and not anything in the bilateral channel, is where confirmation would actually draw blood.

The bind in Brussels

The European layer is where I think most coverage has the shape of the problem wrong, in an interesting way.

The EU now has, for the first time, a legal framework covering exactly this scenario. The Crisis and Force Majeure Regulation, part of the migration pact that came into application this summer, defines the instrumentalisation of migrants and attaches a procedure to it. The framework was written with Belarus in mind, after Minsk manufactured arrivals into Poland and Lithuania in 2021, and with Russia as the assumed second case. Both are declared adversaries, which made the politics easy.

Two features of the instrument matter here, and they are not the ones being discussed.

First, it is not a sanctions regime. Recognising instrumentalisation does not, by itself, cost the instrumentalising state anything. What it does is allow the member state under pressure to derogate from normal asylum and border procedures for a period, extending registration deadlines and border procedures, with enhanced solidarity from the rest of the bloc. It manages the victim's obligations. Any political or economic cost to the responsible state, from trade measures to formal censure, would require separate decisions on separate legal bases. Formally classifying the episode as instrumentalisation by Rabat, and then attaching costs to that finding, are two distinct steps, and each would be unprecedented against a state holding an association agreement and preferential access to the EU market.

Second, and this is the detail I keep coming back to, the procedure starts with the member state itself. A reasoned request goes from the affected government to the Commission, which assesses it and proposes derogations for the Council to approve. Nobody in Brussels can classify the Ceuta crisis as Moroccan instrumentalisation unless Madrid asks. And Madrid, for every reason set out above, is the capital least able to afford asking. Spain, and indirectly the other member states worried about onward movement, depends more heavily on Moroccan cooperation than any of the countries the regulation was written to protect ever depended on Belarus.

So the mechanism exists, the definition plausibly fits, and the state holding the trigger is structurally unable to pull it.

The regulation's own thresholds add a further layer: instrumentalisation requires showing encouragement or facilitation of the movements with the aim of destabilising the Union or a member state. Officers observing a response would not meet that bar on their own. The crossings themselves might, if direction were ever proven, but direction is precisely the claim nothing yet supports.

This is the double standard the episode would expose if the claim held. Europe built a tool for migration as coercion and calibrated it, in practice, for enemies. Confronted with the same behaviour from a partner it cannot alienate, the union would discover that the tool points only at states it was already prepared to punish. Belarus earned the framework by being Belarus. Morocco would be protected from it by being needed.

The guarantee nobody wants to specify

There is an older question underneath all of this, and hostile intelligence activity on Ceutan soil would sharpen it whether or not anyone in Madrid or Brussels wants it sharpened.

The prevailing legal reading is that Ceuta and Melilla sit outside NATO's Article 5, because Article 6 of the Washington Treaty defines the covered area as the parties' territory in Europe and North America, and the two cities are in Africa. The EU's mutual assistance clause, Article 42.7 TEU, has no such geographic carve-out and covers member state territory as such. On paper, then, the two cities are more European than Atlantic.

But territorial coverage and legal threshold are separate questions. Article 42.7 is triggered by armed aggression, and nothing in last week's events — not the crossings, not alleged observers, not the deaths — plausibly reaches that threshold. Neither defence clause is a clean fit for what actually happened, which is precisely the point. The two cities' security rests in practice on Spanish national means and on Moroccan restraint, and the second of these is the variable under discussion. Confirmation would not activate anything. It would simply make it harder to keep pretending the question is settled.

Rabat's own arithmetic

The last piece is Morocco's side of the ledger, and it runs against escalation from that direction too.

Rabat is currently negotiating a replacement fisheries arrangement with Brussels, after the EU Court of Justice annulled the previous fisheries and trade agreements in October 2024 over Western Sahara. It wants continued EU funding. Above all it is protecting a Western Sahara position that has been improving steadily for years, with Spain's 2022 endorsement as one of the trophies. A public fight with Madrid, or confirmation of an intelligence operation inside a mass casualty event, serves none of those interests. If the claim is ever pressed formally, the likeliest Moroccan posture is flat denial followed by quiet de-escalation at the fence, which, it is worth noting, is roughly what Spanish officials say has already happened since Friday, with Moroccan forces moving to stop further gatherings on their side.

Which suggests a closing irony worth sitting with. If the observers were real, their masters' interests and their victims' interests now point in the same direction: bury it. Madrid cannot afford the confrontation, Rabat does not want the exposure, and Brussels lacks a trigger anyone is willing to pull. Confirmation would change public understanding of what happened last week. I am much less sure it would change anything else.
